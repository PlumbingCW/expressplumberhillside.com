Sewage in a floor drain, a toilet that will not flush and a tub filling from below are the signs of a failing main line, and emergency sewer line repair in Hillside starts with a stop to water use. Do not flush, shower or run the washer. Then call the 24/7 line. We schedule same-day camera work when a plumber is available.

A sewer line that fails is not always a dramatic collapse. Most are slow: a joint offsets, roots work in, a low spot collects waste. The backup is the day the slow problem becomes a fast one.

When a sewer backup is an emergency

Sewage is a health hazard and it keeps rising while fixtures run. Do these things until the plumber arrives.

Stop all water useNo flushing, showers, dishwasher or laundry. Every gallon has nowhere to go.
Keep people and pets outSewage water carries bacteria. Keep children away from wet floors and drains.
Do not use chemical cleanersThey do not clear a failed pipe and they make the work unsafe for the plumber.
Photograph and note the timeYour insurer and the plumber will both ask when it started and what backed up.

How we diagnose the line

We run a camera through the cleanout or a toilet flange and watch the pipe from inside. The camera shows roots, an offset joint, a crushed section, a belly holding water or scale narrowing the bore. A locator at the surface marks the depth and the spot so a repair dig, if needed, is small.

This matters because the right fix depends on the failure. A single cracked joint is a spot repair. A line that is cracked end to end is a replacement. Guessing wastes money in both directions, so we show you the footage and explain it.

Repair options: dig, line or replace

Trenchless lining and pipe bursting avoid a long trench but suit only some pipes. They need a line that is continuous enough to line, and a specialist who can say honestly whether yours qualifies. Excavation is more disruptive and it is also the surest fix when the pipe has collapsed or has a bad belly.

  • Spot repair: one failed joint or section is cut out and replaced.
  • Lining: a resin sleeve is cured inside a sound but leaking pipe.
  • Replacement: the whole run from house to the connection is renewed.
  • Cleanout installation: gives future access so cameras and jets can reach the line.

Why Hillside lines fail

More than 79 percent of Hillside homes were built before 1960. Many sewer laterals from that era are clay or cast iron, both of which crack, separate or grow a rough interior over decades. Street trees send roots to the joints. Two-family buildings add heavier daily flow to an old pipe sized for less.

Pipe past its working life tends to fail in clusters. If one joint has opened, the neighbors are often close behind. That is why the camera run covers the whole lateral, not only the spot where the backup began.

Who owns which part of the sewer

The lateral from your house to the connection at the street is generally the property owner's responsibility. Ask the Hillside Building and Housing Department at (973) 926-5100 how connection and permit rules apply to your lot before any excavation. Digging in a public street or sidewalk takes municipal approval, and the plumber should not start without it.

What affects the price

  • Depth and length of the failed section.
  • Pipe material and whether lining is possible.
  • What sits above the pipe: lawn, driveway, concrete or a finished floor.
  • Whether permits or street work are required.
  • Whether the visit is after hours.

You hear the price before work starts. If a line has only a clog and not a failure, drain cleaning is the lower cost first step, and a camera look through sewer inspection tells you which case you have. Many homeowners policies exclude sewer line wear, so ask your insurer, and call (908) 357-1542 for your price.

Standards and sources behind this page

Work described here falls under New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code, whose plumbing subcode is the National Standard Plumbing Code, 2021 New Jersey edition, according to the New Jersey Division of Codes and Standards. Digging also brings in the New Jersey One Call call, which marks buried utilities before excavation.
