Irvington is a dense township of about 2.9 square miles, split between old single-family houses and many two- and three-family buildings. That mix shapes what plumbers are called for. A drain backing up in one unit often traces to a stack or a main line shared by several.

The calls Irvington homes make most

Start with the drains. In a building where two or three households share one stack, a clogged kitchen line shows up in the first floor tub. We clear it through drain cleaning with cable equipment, then check the main with a camera so the clog does not return.

Second is water that should not be there. The Elizabeth River runs through the township, and basements near it can take on water in a heavy rain. A failed sump pump or a floor drain that backs up is a sump pump and sewer problem, not only a drain problem. Third are water heaters and boilers in basements that were built in earlier decades.

Why housing age matters here

About half of Irvington's housing units were built before 1960, according to the Census Bureau's American Community Survey as summarized by MyTownView, and nearly a quarter go back to 1939 or earlier. Pipe from that period is often cast iron, clay or galvanized steel. Each one fails in its own way: cast iron roughens, clay admits roots and galvanized closes up from the inside.

That is why we ask about the building before we quote. A two-family house with its original basement stack needs a different plan than a 1990s townhouse. When supply lines are the problem, repiping is the honest answer, and we will say so.

Many Irvington buildings hold two or three units on one set of pipes. When one unit calls about a leak, the cause may be in a neighbor's unit or in a shared line.
